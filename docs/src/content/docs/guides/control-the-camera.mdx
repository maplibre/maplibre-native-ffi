The camera is the familiar one: center, zoom, bearing, pitch, plus roll, field
of view, padding, and center altitude. It lives on the map, not on the render
session, so you can move it before anything is drawing. Transitions advance as
you pump the runtime.

## Fields you set, fields you get

Camera options are a **field mask plus values**. Only the fields flagged in
`fields` are applied, so setting the zoom leaves the center alone — there is no
read-modify-write dance and no need to fill in values you do not care about.
Reading the camera gives you a snapshot with the current values filled in.

Screen positions are in **logical pixels**, origin top-left — the same units as
the map's `width` and `height`, never device pixels.

One field is input-only: `anchor`, the screen point a camera command pivots
around. Snapshots never report it back.

## Jump, ease, fly

Three commands apply camera options, and the difference between them is time:

* `jump_to` — immediate.
* `ease_to` — eased, but the default duration is **zero**. Without an explicit
  `duration_ms` it behaves exactly like a jump.
* `fly_to` — the one command that animates by default. Left alone it derives a
  duration from how far it is flying, at 1.2 screenfuls per second.

Animation options also take a cubic easing curve, and `cancel_transitions()`
stops whatever is in flight.

## Gestures

Pan, zoom, rotate, and pitch come in relative form, for wiring straight to input
events: `move_by` (screen-space pan), `scale_by` (zoom by a factor around an
optional anchor), `rotate_by` (rotate between two screen points), and `pitch_by`
(a delta in degrees). Each has an animated variant with the same zero-duration
default as `ease_to`.

Absolute targets go through `jump_to`, `ease_to`, or `fly_to`.

## Fit the camera to content

The `camera_for_*` family computes a camera that fits something into the current
viewport — a bounding box, a list of coordinates, or a geometry — and hands it
back without applying it. Pass the result to a transition to actually move
there. Fit options add padding, bearing, and pitch. Going the other way,
`lat_lng_bounds_for_camera()` reports the bounds a camera covers.

Because these answer against the current viewport, the map's size matters:
before you attach a render session, that is the `width` and `height` you created
the map with.

## Constrain the camera

`mln_map_set_bounds()` limits where the camera may go — geographic bounds,
min/max zoom, min/max pitch — using the same field-mask pattern. Paired limits
have to be consistent with each other.

## Screen and world coordinates

`pixel_for_lat_lng` and `lat_lng_for_pixel` convert against the live map, with
batch variants for many points at once.

For overlays, `mln_map_projection_create()` is often the better tool: it freezes
the map's current transform into a standalone projection object that keeps
answering conversions even as the camera moves on, and even after the map is
gone. That is what you want when placing UI on top of a frame you already
rendered.

`projected_meters_for_lat_lng` and its inverse are stateless spherical Mercator
conversions needing no map at all. Metrics like meters-per-pixel are arithmetic
on top of those.

## Watch the camera move

Transitions emit `MAP_CAMERA_WILL_CHANGE`, `MAP_CAMERA_IS_CHANGING`, and
`MAP_CAMERA_DID_CHANGE`. Read `mln_map_get_camera()` during `IS_CHANGING` to
mirror an animating camera into your own UI.
